Pedestrian road injuries in Australia, 2011–2021

The hidden hospital burden of pedestrian injuries

… hospital beds filled by injured pedestrians on an average day in 2021

Pedestrians are only a small share of the people admitted to hospital after road crashes. But they stay much longer than most, and that is easy to miss if we only count cases.

Part A · Why do stays last so long?

Story 1 · Older people

Each of their admissions lasted … days. Within every adult age group, pedestrians also stayed 1.7 to 2.7 times longer than car drivers of the same age, so this is not only an age effect.

What can be done

Give slower walkers time to cross. Older adults walk about 0.77 m per second on average, well below the 1.2 m per second that most crossings assume (Western et al., 2025). Longer crossing times, pedestrian detection at signals, and awareness messages aimed at drivers and older people all target this group.

Story 2 · Heavier vehicles

The heavier the vehicle, the longer the stay

Between 2016 and 2021, a pedestrian hit by a car, ute or van stayed … days per admission. One hit by a truck or bus stayed … days, and one hit by a train …. Trucks and buses caused only … of pedestrian admissions but … of their bed days. Choose an age group: the pattern holds in each one.

What can be done

Reduce crash energy. For trucks on city streets, that means blind-spot systems, side underrun protection and automatic emergency braking, which Australia's Safer Freight Vehicles package encourages (DITRDCA, 2023). For cars, which cause … of cases, it means lower speeds where people walk: the risk of death rises steeply with impact speed (Hussain et al., 2019).

Part B · Why are there so many cases?

Story 3 · Winter

Pedestrian admissions rise every winter

Averaged over 2011–2019, pedestrian admissions in winter were … above an average season and summer admissions … below, while road injuries overall were slightly below average in winter. Pedestrians' share of road-injury admissions rose from … in summer to … in winter, and winter was higher than summer in all … years from 2011 to 2021. Winter brings dark evenings, but also holidays and weather, so this is a pattern, not proof of a cause.

What can be done

Help drivers see people on foot. Street lighting can prevent road injuries, though the studies are of mixed quality (Beyer & Ker, 2009). Lighting at crossings and campaigns from autumn into winter, aimed at drivers and pedestrians, match the season when cases rise (RAA, 2025).

Story 4 · Children and young people

For young children, one road injury in six happens on foot

People under 40 make up … of pedestrian admissions. For children aged 0–7, … of all their road-injury admissions were pedestrians, three times the all-ages share of …. Their stays are short, so this story is about the number of cases, not bed days.

What can be done

Aim awareness at the right people. Texting while walking is linked to less looking before crossing (Simmons et al., 2020), so messages for young adults can focus on phones at crossings; for young children the audience is their parents. Campaigns work best alongside other measures: mass media alone have shown little effect on crashes (Phillips et al., 2011).

Count bed days, not just cases

Ranking road users by admissions alone under-ranks pedestrians. A 30% cut in pedestrian bed days, in line with the national target for serious injuries (Australian Government, 2021), would free about … hospital beds every day.

About the data

Hospital data come from the Bureau of Infrastructure and Transport Research Economics' national hospitalised injury workbook (BITRE, 2023), Traffic rows, calendar years 2011–2021. Population for rates comes from ABS Table 59, June 2021 (ABS, 2026). All processing was done in a KNIME workflow; the website loads its output tables unchanged.

Limitations

  • Admissions count hospital separations, not people.
  • Counting changed three times: Victoria in 2012, bed-day rules in July 2015, and NSW in 2017. The big-picture chart marks each change.
  • The 2021 death fields are blank, so the check without patients who died covers 2011–2020 only.
  • The data show hospital use, not the causes of crashes.
  • The responses come from the cited research, not from these data.
